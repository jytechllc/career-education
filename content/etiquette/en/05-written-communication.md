Text carries no tone and no facial expression. The reader gets exactly what you wrote and nothing else. Written clearly, one message saves everyone's time; written vaguely, it generates three rounds of clarification. Over a year that gap becomes a reputation.

## 1. Email: structure is the whole game

**The subject line** should let someone triage from the inbox list:

- Weak: "Some questions about the project"
- Strong: "[Action needed] Q3 channel budget — please confirm by Thursday"

Tagging the action type (`[Action needed]`, `[FYI]`, `[Done]`) and putting the deadline in the subject is a high-return habit.

**The body**

1. **Lead with the ask or the conclusion**: "Could you approve the budget below by Thursday?"
2. **Then the context**, three to five lines.
3. **List what you need**, one action per bullet.
4. **Describe attachments** rather than leaving them to be discovered: "Attached is the detail sheet; the second tab has the channel split."

**To, Cc, Bcc**

- **To = people who must act.** If several people are in To, nobody owns it — name them: "Wang to approve the budget, Li to check section two."
- **Cc = people who need to know but not act.** Cc'ing someone's manager to apply pressure is visible for what it is.
- **Bcc** is for protecting recipient privacy on external sends. Using it to let a third party silently read a thread costs you a great deal of trust when discovered.
- **Pause before Reply All.** Does everyone need your "Got it"?

**Tone.** Keep courtesy to one line and get to the point. Useful patterns:

- Opening: `Hi Sarah,` / `Dear Dr. Chen,` (formal)
- Ask: `Could you confirm the Q3 budget by Thursday?`
- Close: `Thanks,` / `Best regards,`
- Nudge: `Following up on my note below — is there anything you need from me to move this forward?` (much softer than "As I mentioned earlier")

## 2. Chat: Slack, Teams, WeChat

The core tension: **chat is cheapest for the sender and most disruptive for the receiver.** Most of the etiquette exists to correct that asymmetry.

- **Never send just "hi" and wait.** Put the question in the first message: "Hi Wang — checking the integration slot for Wednesday: does morning or afternoon work for you?"
- **One message per thought.** Splitting a paragraph across seven sends means seven notifications.
- **Be careful with voice notes.** Fastest for you, slowest for the listener — not searchable, not skimmable, unusable in a meeting. Type for managers and clients. If you must, keep it under twenty seconds and add a text summary.
- **@-mention precisely.** Use @channel only when it genuinely concerns everyone.
- **Match emoji to the room.** Relaxed on your team channel, restrained with clients. Note that some symbols read differently across generations and regions — when unsure, use words.

**Time boundaries**

- Send non-urgent messages during working hours; almost every tool supports scheduled send, so use it.
- If you must write after hours, say so: "Not urgent — tomorrow is fine."
- Conversely, **you are not obliged to answer instantly outside working hours** unless your role carries an on-call expectation. Clarify which it is when you join.

**When to stop typing and call**: more than three rounds without resolution, anything involving emotion or disagreement, or genuine urgency. **Re-explaining in text something that would take three minutes by voice is the most common time sink in remote work.**

## 3. Habits that prevent a lot of trouble

- **Put decisions in writing.** After a call: "Confirming: option B, delivery Friday, I'll own the handoff. Correct me if I've got that wrong." This is standard practice, not distrust.
- **Reread before sending** — recipients, amounts, dates and attachments are where mistakes cost most.
- **Never send angry.** Save the draft, look again in twenty minutes. You will change it nearly every time.
- **Assume anything you write may be forwarded.** It often is. This single assumption fixes your wording, your claims and your urge to vent about a colleague.
- **Acknowledge receipt.** "Got it — you'll have it Thursday" costs nothing and stops the other person from having to track you.

## Common mistakes

- **Red, bold, all caps for urgency.** All caps reads as shouting. If it's genuinely urgent, call.
- **Pasting a long document into the body.** Attach or link; keep the body to the summary and the ask.
- **Escalating by Cc.** It may work once; after that people route around you.
- **Forwarding content — especially screenshots — without permission.** Ask first. It is basic trust maintenance.

---

**One line to remember**: the only test for written communication is whether the reader finishes it **knowing what they need to do and by when.**
