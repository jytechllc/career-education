Most people meet workplace etiquette as a pile of rules: who offers a hand first, where to stand in the elevator, how low to hold your glass. Those rules have answers, but anyone who only memorizes answers will fail the moment they hit a situation the handbook never covered.

What actually holds up is judgment. It breaks down into three usable principles.

## 1. Etiquette exists to lower the other person's cost

Strip the form off any polite act and what remains is this: **you absorbed a cost so someone else didn't have to.**

- A subject line with a clear ask saves the reader from hunting for the point.
- Arriving five minutes early saves a room full of people from waiting.
- Adding your team and current project to an introduction saves the other person from guessing who you are and why you matter to them.

So before you act, ask: **does this save me effort, or save them effort?** If the answer is "me," you are usually at the start of a misstep. Forwarding a document with no note, sending "hey, you there?" at midnight, dumping twenty voice messages — all of these move the work of interpretation onto someone else.

## 2. Judgment = relationship distance × formality of the setting

The same sentence can be warm or out of line depending on two variables and only two.

| | Informal setting | Formal setting |
|---|---|---|
| **Close** (your immediate team) | First names, light jokes | Titles still apply; keep inside jokes out |
| **Distant** (other teams, clients, skip-level) | Friendly but not familiar | Formal address, business topics only |

The classic mistake is importing a style that works in one quadrant into another — joking in a client meeting the way you joke with your pod, or carrying the tone of an offsite conversation into Monday's formal review.

**Warmth should be initiated by the more senior party.** If a director tells you to use their first name, use it. If a client opens up about family, you can follow. Crossing that line first puts all of the risk on you.

## 3. Boundaries: safe, careful, off-limits

A working relationship is not a friendship, and sorting topics into three tiers avoids most awkwardness:

- **Safe** — work, industry news, commuting, food, sports, travel, films, pets.
- **Careful** — salary, relationship status, age, appearance, children, family background. Not forbidden, but only safe when the other person raises them and you mostly listen.
- **Off-limits** — politics, religion, other people's private matters, opinions about colleagues, confidential business information.

Two traps catch newcomers most often: **asking about pay** (many companies treat it as confidential and you put the other person in a bind) and **evaluating colleagues** (assume every word gets back to them, because it usually does).

## 4. What to do after you get it wrong

Etiquette is not a zero-error game. What people remember is rarely the mistake — it is how you handled it.

1. **Fix the thing; don't perform the apology.** "Sorry — I read the wrong column. Corrected version in ten minutes" beats three rounds of "I'm so sorry, I'm so careless."
2. **Don't build a chain of explanations.** One sentence of context is fine. Continuing into how busy and tired you were reads as deflection.
3. **Don't repeat it.** The first time is a slip; the second is a pattern, and tolerance for the two is very different.

## Common mistakes

- **Confusing deference with politeness.** Endless "whatever you prefer" pushes the decision onto someone else, which is more cost, not less.
- **Reserving manners for senior people.** Deferential upward and careless downward is the most transparent and most damaging posture there is. The receptionist, the cleaner, the contractor and the vendor get the same standard.
- **Treating "I'm just blunt" as a licence.** Directness is about saying what you actually think, not about skipping the work of saying it well.
