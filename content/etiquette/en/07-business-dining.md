A business meal is never about the food. It is about building trust in a setting looser than a conference room. So the measure of success isn't how precisely the ritual was followed — it's **whether everyone at the table was comfortable**, especially the guest of honor and whoever is least at ease socially.

## 1. Chinese banquets

**Seating**

- The seat **facing the door** is the host's, taken by the most senior person on the hosting side.
- The **principal guest** sits to the host's right, the second guest to the left.
- The **deputy host** sits opposite, near the service entrance, and handles ordering, staff and the bill.
- Practical advice: **wait to be seated.** Taking the host's chair is a conspicuous error.

**Ordering**

- The host orders, but **asks about restrictions first** — allergies, religious requirements (halal, vegetarian), medical needs. That question matters far more than the dishes.
- Balance meat, vegetables, soup and a staple; order slightly more than needed, never exactly enough.
- Offering the menu to the principal guest for a dish or two is standard courtesy; if they decline, don't press.

**At the table**

- Use **serving chopsticks and spoons** — now the norm. Provide them as well as use them.
- Turn the lazy Susan clockwise, **guest of honor first**, and check that nobody is mid-reach.
- Don't place food in someone's bowl with your own chopsticks; use the serving set or ask for plated portions.
- Phones off the table; take calls outside.
- Keep the conversation light. Substantive business is usually handled before or after the meal, not mid-toast.

**Toasting**

- The host toasts first, then others in order of seniority. When toasting someone senior, **hold your glass rim slightly below theirs.**
- Say something specific — thanks for the collaboration, wishes for the project — rather than "I'll finish mine, you do as you like."
- **Do not push drinks on anyone.** In China this is no longer only a matter of manners: pressuring someone into drinking has produced real liability findings, and organizers carry the most exposure.

**Declining a drink**

- Be direct, brief, and offer a substitute: "I'm on medication, so tea for me tonight — let me toast you first."
- "I'm driving" and "doctor's orders" are widely accepted and need no elaboration.
- What actually matters is **the gesture**: stand, hold the glass with both hands, look at the person, say your line. Do that and almost nobody registers what's in the glass.
- As host, provide non-alcoholic options and say up front that nobody is expected to drink.

**The bill**: settle it in advance or step away quietly. Letting guests witness a scramble over the check is an organizing failure.

## 2. Western and international settings

- **Work cutlery from the outside in**, one set per course. Knife and fork together at roughly four o'clock signals you're finished; apart means pausing.
- **Napkin** on your lap once seated, on your chair if you step away, to the left of the plate at the end.
- **Bread** is torn into pieces and buttered one at a time, not bitten whole.
- **Spoon soup away from you**, quietly. Audible eating is a clear negative in Western settings.
- **A toast** is usually a short word from the host; everyone raises a glass, touches or nods, and takes a sip. There is no expectation to empty the glass.
- **Splitting the bill is common socially** in the US and Europe and implies nothing about respect. In business settings, the inviting party still pays.
- **Tipping varies**: roughly 18–20% in US restaurants, none in Japan, and service usually included in much of Europe. Check local practice before you travel.

## 3. Receptions, mixers and company parties

The hard part here isn't manners — it's **starting and ending conversations.**

**Opening**: use the shared situation. "That talk was interesting — what did you make of it?" Don't lead with a card and a pitch.

**Sustaining**: ask more than you tell. Letting the other person talk makes you more memorable, not less.

**Closing** — the step most people skip. Say a clear line and move:

> "It was good talking — I'm going to go say hello to a few people. I'll follow up online."

Never let a conversation die by silence or by checking your phone.

Other details:

- Hold your glass in your left hand so your right stays dry for handshakes.
- Stand open to the room; in a group, leave a gap so others can join.
- Take part in party games without forcing anyone else to; if you're organizing, avoid anything involving physical contact or personal disclosure.
- **Getting drunk is the one hard line.** A single loss of composure can undo years of professional credit. Set yourself a limit and switch to water when you hit it.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating dinner as the negotiation.** Terms agreed late in the evening get re-examined by both sides in the morning.
- **Attending only to the guest of honor.** Look after everyone, especially the quietest person at the table.
- **Pressing food on people.** Repeatedly filling someone's bowl and asking why they aren't eating creates pressure.
- **Ignoring dietary restrictions.** Seating a Muslim colleague in front of a table of pork is both unrecoverable and entirely avoidable.

---

**One line to remember**: a business meal is judged not by how many toasts you gave, but by whether **everyone left feeling looked after.**
