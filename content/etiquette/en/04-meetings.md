A meeting is the setting where the largest number of colleagues observe you at the same time. Whether you come across as competent depends not only on what you say, but on how you listen, how you wait, and how you close.

## 1. Before

- **Arrive five to ten minutes early.** In person that means picking a seat and testing the display; on video it means confirming your mic and connection. Arriving exactly on time isn't late, but it leaves no margin.
- **Read the pre-read.** Walking in without having opened the doc shows up in your first question.
- **Know your role** — decision-maker, presenter, contributor, or observer. Saying little is fine for an observer and a failure for a presenter.
- **Prepare the screen.** If you're sharing, close unrelated windows, silence notifications and hide the bookmarks bar. **A personal message popping up mid-share is one of the most common self-inflicted workplace incidents.**

## 2. Seating

Internal meetings are usually informal, but two things still help: don't take the far corner from the screen (it reads as disengaged), and don't sit by the door and then walk in and out repeatedly.

**Formal or client meetings, especially in China**, follow a seating order: the seat facing the door is the host position, the principal guest sits to the host's right, and each side arranges by seniority outward. **When in doubt, wait to be seated** — taking the host's chair is a conspicuous error.

## 3. During

**Speaking**

- **Let people finish.** Interrupting is the most damaging move available in a meeting. When you must, flag it: "Can I jump in for one second to check a number?"
- **Conclusion first, reasoning second.** "My recommendation is not to build it, for two reasons…" beats a five-minute wind-up.
- **Watch the clock.** If a single point runs past two minutes internally, it probably belongs in a follow-up.
- **Disagree with the idea, never the person.** "I read that metric differently" and "your idea doesn't hold up" land very differently.

**Listening**

- Take notes, even keywords.
- Nod and make eye contact so the speaker knows they have you.
- **Phone face down or away.** Looking at it is visible to everyone in the room.
- If you truly must deal with something urgent, say so: "Apologies — one urgent message, two minutes."

**Closing**

The most-wasted minute of any meeting is the last one. Build the habit of confirming three things before people leave: **what was decided, who owns it, and by when.** If nobody else does this, do it — it is a cheap action with outsized returns.

## 4. Video meetings

- **Camera** — follow the team default. On for one-on-ones, client calls and anything consequential.
- **Light and background** — face the window, not away from it. A plain wall beats a virtual background that tears when bandwidth drops.
- **Look at the lens** when you speak, not at the faces on screen. It noticeably improves the sense that you're addressing people.
- **Mute when you're not speaking**, and **unmute before you start talking** — the three-words-then-realize routine is the single most common waste on video calls.
- **On a bad connection**, drop video and keep audio: "My connection is unstable, I'll go camera-off."
- **To interject in a large call**, use the raise-hand button or the chat rather than talking over someone.
- **Always ask before recording**, and say what the recording is for and who will see it. In many jurisdictions this is a legal requirement, not just a courtesy.

**Across time zones**, always state the zone ("Thu 10:00 CST / Wed 19:00 ET") and take turns absorbing the inconvenient slot. On distributed teams this is noticed and valued.

## 5. Presenting and being questioned

- **If you don't know, say so and commit to a time**: "I don't have that number with me — I'll send it to the channel within the hour." Improvising numbers is the fastest way to spend down your credibility.
- **When challenged, don't defend immediately.** Clarify the concern first: "Is the worry the metric definition, or the data source?" The gap is usually smaller than it sounded.
- **If a manager rejects your proposal in the room**, accept it briefly and follow up privately with detail. Arguing it to a win in public usually loses more than it gains.

## Common mistakes

- **Working on something else during the call.** Typing, drifting eyes and "sorry, could you repeat that?" are all audible.
- **Assuming silence is safe.** People who never speak are easily read as contributing nothing. One good question counts as participation.
- **Not following through afterwards.** Failing to do what you agreed to is worse than any etiquette slip.
- **Calling a meeting with no agenda.** Inviting people without saying what will be discussed offloads all the preparation onto them.

---

**One line to remember**: what people actually remember is whether **you moved the discussion forward** — and whether **you did what you said you would afterwards.**
