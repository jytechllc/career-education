Cross-cultural mistakes rarely come from rudeness. They come from **carrying your defaults into a system built on different ones.** What reads as modest and steady in one workplace can read as lacking a point of view in another; feedback that is efficient in one can look like a loss of face in another.

One caveat before anything else: **everything below describes tendencies, not people.** Use it to explain why a reaction surprised you — never to predict what a specific individual will do.

## 1. The dimensions that matter

**How direct communication is**

- **High-context** (China, Japan, Korea, parts of Southeast Asia and the Middle East): meaning sits in context, tone and what goes unsaid. "We'll study it further" or "that may be difficult" usually means no.
- **Low-context** (US, Germany, Netherlands, Nordics, Israel): meaning is largely literal. No means no; "sounds good" means agreement.

**How criticism is delivered**

- **US**: wrapped in positives but substantively direct. When you hear "This is interesting — have you considered…", the point is in the second half.
- **German, Dutch**: blunt, with no cushioning, and **not personal**. Being told flatly that something doesn't work is not hostility.
- **Chinese, Japanese**: indirect and face-preserving; negative views often arrive privately or through a third party.

**How decisions get made**

- **US**: top-down and fast, adjusted in flight.
- **Japan**: extensive pre-alignment (*nemawashi*) and group consensus — slow to decide, fast to execute.
- **China**: clear hierarchy; what matters is whether the decision-maker is convinced, and the pre-meeting conversation often matters more than the meeting.
- **Nordics, Netherlands**: flat and consensus-driven; junior people are expected to speak.

**Time**

- Germany, Switzerland, Japan: punctuality equals reliability; five minutes late needs an explanation.
- US: punctual for business, looser socially.
- Parts of southern Europe, Latin America, the Middle East and South Asia: more elastic — but **as the outsider, still be on time.** Nobody criticizes punctuality; lateness gets remembered.

## 2. Four things that most often cost Chinese professionals in US workplaces

1. **Not stating your own contribution.** US performance systems assume you will describe your results. Waiting to be noticed usually means waiting. Saying what you did and what came of it in a weekly update or a one-on-one is not boasting.
2. **Staying silent in meetings.** Silence is not read as modesty; it is read as having no view or no investment. Asking a question or restating and confirming someone's point both count as participation.
3. **Excessive modesty.** "I didn't do it very well" and "I just got lucky" are taken literally on a resume or in an interview. Replace evaluation with fact: "I owned that funnel; retention went from 20% to 32%."
4. **Not saying no.** Agreeing to everything and then slipping is far worse than saying up front, "That won't fit this week — something has to give."

## 3. Specific behaviors worth knowing

**Japan**

- Cards (*meishi*) are given and received with both hands, read carefully, and laid on the table — never folded or written on in front of the giver.
- A nod or a shallow bow is plenty for a foreign visitor.
- Pour for others, not for yourself; steady your glass with a hand when someone pours for you.
- "*Kentō shimasu*" ("we'll consider it") is usually a polite decline.

**Korea**

- Seniority and age are explicit. Receive items, pour and toast with both hands, or with the left hand supporting the right forearm.
- Turning slightly away when drinking with someone older is a traditional mark of respect.

**Germany, Netherlands, Nordics**

- Meetings follow the agenda with little small talk. Preparation matters more than rapport.
- Titles still carry weight in formal German settings, though tech has largely moved to first names.
- Work and private life are firmly separated; out-of-hours messages generally go unanswered, and shouldn't be expected to be answered.

**Middle East, India**

- Use the right hand to give, receive and eat.
- In some settings men and women do not shake hands by default — wait for the other person to offer.
- During Ramadan, avoid eating or drinking in front of fasting colleagues during daylight.

## 4. Four safe moves when you can't tell

1. **Observe before acting.** Spend your first two weeks watching how people address each other, when they arrive, who speaks first, what a status update looks like.
2. **Ask rather than guess.** "I want to check — is it better here to raise a disagreement in the meeting, or afterwards one-on-one?" Asking costs nothing; guessing wrong costs something.
3. **Start formal and relax from there.** Match the more formal end on first contact and follow the other person's lead downward. The reverse is hard to recover from.
4. **Apologize specifically.** If you do get it wrong, say you weren't familiar with the local practice, apologize, and invite correction. Nearly everyone accepts this, and it often builds rapport.

## Common mistakes

- **Using stereotypes to predict individuals.** "Japanese colleagues are indirect" fails the first time you meet a blunt Japanese manager.
- **Equating English fluency with communication.** Structure and a clear bottom line matter far more than vocabulary.
- **Staying silent in the meeting and objecting privately afterwards.** In low-context cultures that reads as consent, and the decision won't be reopened.
- **Ignoring time zones and holidays.** Sending an urgent request during Lunar New Year, Thanksgiving, Christmas, Ramadan or Obon is easily avoided.

---

**One line to remember**: cross-cultural competence isn't a hundred rules — it is **assuming your defaults don't travel**, then observing, asking and adjusting.
