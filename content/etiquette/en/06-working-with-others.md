Earlier modules covered form. This one covers relationships. Most workplace friction is not caused by bad manners — it is caused by mismatched expectations. What you consider "keeping people posted" and what your manager considers it may differ by a week.

## 1. Upward: remove the need to chase you

**Three principles for reporting**

1. **Bad news first, and early.** Managers can absorb problems; what they can't absorb is learning about one on the day it detonates. A slip flagged in week one still has room to maneuver. Flagged on the deadline, it is only an autopsy.
2. **Bring options, not just problems.** "The vendor slipped a week. Two paths: do front-end integration first, or switch to the backup vendor. I'd take the first — need your call." Now the decision costs your manager ten seconds.
3. **Conclusion first, detail on request.** Give the one-line answer, then ask which part to expand. Their time is usually scarcer than yours.

**Cadence.** Most managers want neither constant approval-seeking nor silence. A workable default: **weekly progress on long work, immediate updates at milestones, immediate flags on changes.** Early in a new job, just ask: "How often would you like updates, and in what format?" The question itself scores points.

**When handed something unreasonable**, don't fight it and don't silently swallow it. Hand the prioritization back:

> "I can take it, but I have A and B due Friday. If this comes first, A slips by two days — how would you like to sequence it?"

**When you disagree with a decision**: argue fully while it is being made, then execute without relitigating. If you believe the risk is serious, record it once in writing — factual, unemotional — and then execute.

## 2. Across: credit before cooperation

The recurring problem in cross-team work is that **what you need isn't what the other team is measured on.**

- **Explain the impact, don't just place an order.** "We need this to set Q3 spend — without it that decision stalls" gets prioritized faster than "can you pull a report?"
- **Give a real deadline and a real priority**, with slack built in. "By end of day Thursday" beats "ASAP."
- **Use the proper channel.** If there's a ticket queue, use it. Pulling strings to jump the line works once and poisons the queue for everyone after.
- **Close the loop.** "We used that data — conversion improved after the reallocation. Thank you." That sentence is worth more than ten generic thank-yous, because it tells someone their work mattered.
- **Bank credit in advance.** Being easy to work with when others need you is what makes you credible when you need them.

**When you disagree, separate facts from interests.** A factual dispute is settled with data. A conflict of interests (two teams with different goals) needs the level that can arbitrate — **and escalating there is the correct path, not tattling**, provided you tell your counterpart first: "Neither of us can decide this, let's bring both leads in."

## 3. Downward: what changes when you manage

Once you lead a team, your offhand remarks acquire weight. "Maybe look at that next week" can be received as a directive.

- **Criticize privately, praise publicly.** Criticism delivered in front of others is remembered as humiliation, not as content.
- **Every assignment needs three things**: the outcome, the deadline, and the hard constraints. Drop any one and both sides pay for the rework.
- **Don't skip levels** in either direction without telling the person in between.
- **Don't ask about private matters** — relationships, family plans, household finances. In many jurisdictions this is not only rude but legally risky.
- **Keep your promises.** Raises, promotions and transfers you committed to and cannot deliver need an explanation before the date, not after. A manager's most expensive asset is being believed.

## 4. Shared space

Small things that people quietly keep score on:

- Keep calls in open-plan areas audible only within a couple of meters; take long ones elsewhere.
- Ask before borrowing, and return things where you found them.
- Leave shared kitchens, fridges and printers as you found them.
- Keep strong-smelling food away from desks.
- When someone is heads-down or wearing headphones, message rather than tap them on the shoulder.
- **Don't come in sick.** Nobody will read staying home as a lack of commitment.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating a good relationship as a reason to skip process.** The closer the colleague, the more worth writing things down — that's what protects the relationship when something goes wrong.
- **Chronic complaining.** Sustained venting in channels and kitchens makes you look like part of the problem.
- **Claiming credit, deflecting blame.** Do either once and people remember for years.
- **Over-promising.** Saying yes to avoid disappointing someone today always costs more than saying "I can't take that on."

---

**One line to remember**: upward, remove the need to **chase you**; across, be someone who is **reliable**; downward, make the **standard explicit.**
